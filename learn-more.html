On beads, and the women who spoke through them

Nguni beadwork is an encoding system. Colour, pattern and placement carried meaning for those who shared the code, much as a QR code carries a message for a device that knows how to read it. This project borrows that idea: a hut, a door, and a story encoded in colour.

My starting point for this creative project was a desire to explore my culture, roots and the ways in which our histories are preserved and communicated. Although I did not initially have a clear idea of what the final project would look like, I knew that I wanted to create something that connected me to my cultural heritage. I began thinking about how Nguni communities have historically used beadwork and patterns to tell stories, communicate meaning and carry narratives. I was particularly interested in exploring how these practices are valid and meaningful forms of storytelling in their own right. From there, I began considering how these traditional forms of storytelling could connect with modernity. I did not want to approach digital storytelling as something that replaces older practices, but rather as an opportunity to preserve, reinterpret and bring them into conversation with contemporary forms of expression. This is what *Encoded Narratives* seeks to achieve: a bridge between the old and the new. It is an archive, not only of my people's historical practices, but also of the people and stories behind them. In this instance, I explore the story of Lozikeyi, a figure who is often overlooked in conversations about powerful and influential African women. Through this project, I hope to contribute to the preservation of these histories while exploring new ways of telling and sharing them.
